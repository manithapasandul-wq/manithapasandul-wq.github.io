Aircraft Design Project

Design of a Sailplane with a Semi-Monocoque Wing

Department of Mechanical Engineering · University of Moratuwa

This individual aircraft design project involved the aerodynamic, structural, and manufacturing design of a high-performance sailplane wing for a 300 kg aircraft. The work combined aerofoil analysis, finite-wing performance calculations, longitudinal stability assessment, analytical structural design, material selection, buckling evaluation, CAD modelling, and manufacturing planning. MATLAB, XFLR5, and SolidWorks were used to develop the design from the initial aerodynamic requirements to a complete wing assembly consisting of spars, ribs, stringers, and skin panels.

Aerodynamic Design and Performance Analysis

Aerofoil Selection

The aerodynamic design began with a study of existing high-performance sailplanes to identify suitable ranges for wingspan, wing area, aspect ratio, glide ratio, operating speed, and aerofoil selection. Seven glider aerofoils were then analysed in XFLR5 at the design Reynolds number. Their lift, drag, pitching moment, and lift-to-drag characteristics were compared under the same operating conditions.

The WORTMANN FX 76-MP-120 aerofoil was selected for the main wing because it produced the best aerodynamic efficiency among the evaluated profiles, with a maximum two-dimensional lift-to-drag ratio of approximately 192.6 at an angle of attack of 3.75°.

Finite-Wing Geometry

The effects of finite wings, including downwash, wingtip vortices, and induced drag, were then incorporated into the design. MATLAB programs were developed to compare different aspect ratios and taper ratios by evaluating the finite-wing lift slope and Oswald efficiency factor. An aspect ratio of 26 and a taper ratio of 0.6 were selected, producing an estimated Oswald efficiency factor of 0.777.

The resulting wing geometry included:

  • Wingspan: 16 m
  • Wing area: 9.846 m²
  • Root chord: 1.5385 m
  • Tip chord: 0.9231 m
  • Mean aerodynamic chord: 1.2564 m

Wing Performance

Finite-wing lift and drag curves were developed by combining the aerofoil polar data with induced-drag and fuselage-drag estimations. The maximum finite-wing lift-to-drag ratio was approximately 18.83 at an angle of attack of 5.5°. Therefore, the main-wing incidence angle was set to 5.5°, where the wing produced a lift coefficient of approximately 1.43.

A glider polar curve was generated to examine the relationship between flight speed and sink rate. The tangent method identified a best-glide speed of approximately 17.94 m/s, with a corresponding sink rate of approximately 0.953 m/s.

The best-glide speed of 17.94 m/s from the polar curve agreed closely with the theoretically calculated flight velocity of 17.98 m/s.

Longitudinal Stability

Longitudinal stability was also evaluated by introducing a horizontal stabiliser using the symmetrical WORTMANN FX 76-120 aerofoil. The tail was designed with an area of 3 m² and an aerodynamic-centre separation of 4.5 m, with its setting angle chosen to generate the required negative lift. The centre of gravity was estimated using the SolidWorks aircraft model at approximately 0.48 m behind the main-wing leading edge.

The calculated pitching-moment curve had a negative slope and a positive intercept, indicating longitudinal static stability. The predicted trim angle of approximately 5.75° closely matched the selected main-wing incidence angle.

Structural Design, Load Analysis, and Material Selection

Design Loads

The wing structure was analysed as a cantilever, with one half of the wing considered for the structural calculations. A factor of safety of 1.5 was applied, while the critical aerodynamic loading was based on a design load of 3.8 times the aircraft lift.

An elliptical distribution was assumed for the lift, while the wing weight was distributed linearly according to the changing chord. These distributions were combined to obtain the net vertical load acting along the span. The aerodynamic drag distribution was also estimated, producing a total drag force of approximately 160.27 N at the selected flight condition.

MATLAB was used to calculate the following quantities along the span:

  • Lift and weight distributions
  • Net vertical loading
  • Drag distribution
  • Shear-force distributions
  • Bending-moment distributions
  • Torsional-moment distribution

The aerodynamic pitching moment produced the total torsional moment carried by the wing box. The bending and shear diagrams showed that the highest structural loads occurred near the wing root and reduced toward the tip.

Spar Design and Material Selection

A dual-spar internal structure was selected, using two tapered C-section spars connected through the upper and lower wing skins to form a thin-walled, single-cell wing box. The spar caps were designed to resist the majority of the spanwise bending loads, while the spar webs and skin panels carried shear and contributed to torsional stiffness.

The lumped-area method was used to evaluate the second moment of area and bending stresses in the spar caps, giving compression in the upper caps and tension in the lower caps at the root.

Several aluminium alloys were compared by considering yield strength, shear strength, Young’s modulus, density, estimated structural mass, and wing deflection. Aluminium 5056-H18 was selected for the spars because it provided a suitable balance of low density, high strength, fatigue resistance, formability, and corrosion resistance.

The estimated mass of the two spars before introducing lightening holes was approximately 41.71 kg. The predicted maximum wing-tip deflection was 0.5848 m, remaining below the design limit of 0.8 m — 5% of the wingspan.

Skin, Buckling and Stiffening

The wing skin was analysed as part of the closed-section structure carrying shear and torsional loads. Aluminium 2024-T3 was selected for the 1.5 mm skin panels because of its high strength and stiffness. The calculated maximum shear stress was approximately 5.25 MPa, remaining well below the material shear-strength limit.

Compression buckling of the upper skin was also investigated. Plate-buckling equations were used to determine the allowable spacing between supporting members. Based on the calculated compressive stresses, the number and distribution of stringers were varied along the span to provide greater reinforcement near the highly loaded wing root and fewer stringers toward the wing tip. The ribs maintained the aerofoil profile, supported the skin, transferred local loads, and divided the skin into smaller panels to improve buckling resistance.

Design for Manufacturing and Assembly

Spar Manufacture

The final stage converted the aerodynamic and structural design into a practical manufacturing and assembly plan. Material availability, manufacturing accuracy, component complexity, forming requirements, assembly access, and weight reduction were considered when developing the wing structure.

The front and rear spars were designed to be manufactured from 5 mm Aluminium 5056-H18 sheets. Waterjet cutting was selected to produce the tapered profiles and lightening holes because it provides high dimensional accuracy without introducing significant thermal distortion. After cutting, the spar sheets would be bent along two lines to form the C-section flanges. The springback effect was considered by slightly overbending the material to obtain the required final 90° angle.

Each spar was designed with an initial length of 8 m. Where manufacturing equipment or material dimensions did not allow a continuous spar, the design permitted two 4 m sections to be connected using an overlapping strap joint and riveted connection.

Ribs and Stringers

The ribs were designed from Aluminium 5056-H18 billets and manufactured using CNC milling. The rib CAD model included:

  • Aerofoil-shaped outer geometry
  • Structural flanges
  • Lightening holes
  • Spar connection regions
  • Stringer slots
  • Rivet holes

CNC milling was selected because it could manufacture the complex rib geometry with consistent dimensions and good surface quality. The proposed process included rough machining, finishing, deburring, inspection, and protective surface treatment.

The stringers were designed from 1 mm Aluminium 5056-H18 sheets. The sheet profiles would be cut and bent into the required stiffener cross-section before being positioned through the matching rib slots. The stringers provided spanwise support to the skin, increased structural stiffness, and prevented local skin buckling.

Skin, Joining and Assembly

The external skin panels were specified as 1.5 mm Aluminium 2024-T3 sheets. These panels enclosed the wing box, maintained the aerodynamic surface, and contributed to bending and torsional resistance. Aluminium rivets made from AlMg2.5 material were selected to connect the skins, ribs, spars, and stringers.

The proposed assembly sequence began by aligning the front rib sections with the front spar, followed by the middle and rear rib sections with the rear spar. The stringers were then passed through the rib slots and aligned with the prepared rivet holes. After checking the internal structural alignment, the upper and lower skin panels were attached to complete the closed wing box.

A full three-dimensional SolidWorks assembly was produced to verify component alignment, spar and rib positioning, stringer paths, skin geometry, and overall manufacturability. The final design integrated aerodynamic efficiency, structural strength, buckling resistance, material availability, and practical fabrication requirements into a complete sailplane-wing concept.
