Aircraft Design Project

2m Glider Project

Department of Mechanical Engineering · University of Moratuwa

This project focused on designing and manufacturing a lightweight radio-controlled glider capable of climbing to altitude using a tractor-propeller configuration and then transitioning into an efficient, controlled glide. The aircraft was designed to maintain full control about all three rotational axes through the use of ailerons, elevator, and rudder.

Design, Manufacturing, and Lessons Learned

Aerodynamic Design and Analysis

The design process began with estimating the aircraft weight and calculating the required lift using MATLAB. Several two-dimensional aerofoil profiles were then evaluated using the batch-analysis feature in XFLR5. Lift coefficient, drag coefficient, and lift-to-drag ratio curves were obtained for each aerofoil across a range of angles of attack.

The two-dimensional aerofoil results were subsequently corrected to estimate the finite-wing lift and drag characteristics. Based on the comparison, the NACA 6415 aerofoil was selected because it provided a maximum glide ratio of approximately 21.61 at an angle of attack of 1°.

To improve aerodynamic efficiency, an aspect ratio of 13 was selected for the main wing. A taper ratio of 0.6 was also adopted to increase the Oswald efficiency factor and reduce induced drag. MATLAB calculations indicated that the aircraft required a flight velocity of approximately 6.17 m/s. The ailerons, elevator, and rudder were sized using conventional control-surface proportions relative to the corresponding lifting and stabilising surfaces.

The NACA 6415 aerofoil, an aspect ratio of 13, and a taper ratio of 0.6 together gave a maximum glide ratio of approximately 21.61 at 1° angle of attack — the combination carried forward into the final design.

Structural Design and Manufacturing

The fuselage and tail surfaces were primarily manufactured using low-cost KT board reinforced with 3 mm carbon-fibre rods. This construction method reduced the overall manufacturing cost while providing sufficient strength to withstand aerodynamic loads. It also made the aircraft easier to repair following minor crashes or hard landings.

The main wing was constructed using laser-cut balsa-wood ribs supported by carbon-fibre spars. This arrangement provided a lightweight structure with high bending strength while allowing a moderate level of elastic deflection under aerodynamic loading.

A heat-shrink covering film was used as the wing skin. In addition to producing a smooth aerodynamic surface, the covering increased the rigidity of the wing structure and helped transfer shear loads between the ribs and spars.

Propulsion and Avionics

The aircraft used a four-channel radio-control system consisting of:

  • Throttle
  • Elevator
  • Ailerons
  • Rudder

A Y-harness was used to connect both aileron servos to a single receiver channel. The propulsion system consisted of a 1200 KV brushless outrunner motor, a 30 A electronic speed controller, and a 5200 mAh 3S LiPo battery.

Lessons Learned

The project highlighted the importance of developing a detailed CAD model before fabrication. The initial SolidWorks model lacked sufficient manufacturing detail, which led to several alignment issues during assembly. The two main wing sections also had unequal weights, causing a rolling-balance problem during test flights. In addition, the original design did not include a central spar to support and connect both wings. A new middle spar was therefore introduced to improve structural rigidity and distribute aerodynamic loads and moments more effectively through the fuselage. The tail section was also heavier than expected because the servos were installed near the tail end, affecting the aircraft’s centre of gravity.

These issues demonstrated that successful RC aircraft development requires a detailed model that includes all structural components, equipment locations, estimated masses, centre-of-gravity calculations, and manufacturing tolerances before fabrication begins. The lessons learned from this project were later applied to the development of the 2.5 m fixed-wing UAV, where the structural layout, component placement, weight distribution, wing support, and overall manufacturing accuracy were addressed more carefully during the design and fabrication stages.
