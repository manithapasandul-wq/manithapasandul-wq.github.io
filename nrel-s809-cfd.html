CFD Analysis and Validation of the NREL S809 Aerofoil

Department of Mechanical Engineering · University of Moratuwa

This project replicated the baseline stage of a published study on slotted aerofoils for wind-turbine applications. The work focused on the unslotted NREL S809 aerofoil to establish a validated CFD model that could later be extended to investigate passive flow-control concepts. A two-dimensional, steady-state, incompressible simulation was conducted in ANSYS Fluent using a 600 mm chord aerofoil at a Reynolds number of 1 × 106. The corresponding inlet velocity was 24.34 m/s. Simulations were performed from 0° to 20° angle of attack in 2° increments to evaluate lift, drag, aerodynamic efficiency, pressure distribution, and the development of flow separation.

Method, Results and Validation

Computational Domain and Mesh Development

A large C-type computational domain was created around the aerofoil to reduce the influence of the external boundaries on the flow solution. A structured quadrilateral mesh was developed with concentrated refinement near the aerofoil surface and in the downstream wake region.

Near-wall resolution was controlled using mesh biasing to satisfy the wall-function requirements of the selected turbulence model. The final mesh contained approximately 234,400 elements, with a measured first-layer height of about 0.736 mm. Mesh quality was assessed using skewness, orthogonal quality, element quality, and aspect ratio.

A grid-independence analysis was conducted by systematically increasing the mesh resolution and monitoring changes in the lift and drag coefficients. The aerodynamic coefficients began to stabilise beyond approximately 200,000 elements, so the selected mesh represented a practical balance between computational cost and solution independence.

CFD Setup

The standard k–ε turbulence model with standard wall functions was selected to provide a computationally efficient and numerically stable representation of the external flow. The simulations used:

  • A velocity inlet and pressure outlet
  • A no-slip wall condition on the aerofoil
  • Coupled pressure–velocity solution
  • Second-order pressure and momentum discretisation
  • Least-Squares Cell-Based gradient calculation
  • Residual and aerodynamic-coefficient convergence criteria of 10−4

The angle of attack was changed by resolving the constant inlet velocity into horizontal and vertical components. Lift coefficient, drag coefficient, lift-to-drag ratio, and net mass-flow rate were monitored during every simulation to confirm that a converged solution had been obtained.

Results and Flow Analysis

The lift coefficient increased with angle of attack until reaching a maximum of approximately 1.20 near 14°. Beyond this point the lift began to decrease as flow separation developed and the aerofoil entered the stall region. The maximum lift-to-drag ratio occurred at approximately 6°, the most aerodynamically efficient operating condition within the investigated range.

The drag coefficient increased gradually at lower angles and rose significantly as the angle of attack approached stall.

Velocity and pressure contours were generated for every angle of attack. These contours illustrated:

  • Flow acceleration over the upper aerofoil surface
  • Formation of the pressure difference responsible for lift
  • Growth of the low-velocity wake
  • Progressive movement of the separation region
  • Loss of lift and increased drag near stall

The simulated lift, drag, and lift-to-drag trends were then compared with the published reference data. The lift predictions showed good agreement across most of the tested range, while larger drag deviations occurred at some intermediate and high angles of attack. These differences were associated mainly with mesh distribution, near-wall treatment, turbulence modelling, and the limitations of using a steady-state model close to stall.

Difficulties and Improvements

The main difficulty was obtaining stable convergence at higher angles of attack, where separated flow becomes highly unsteady. More iterations were required near stall, and a steady-state solver could not fully represent the transient nature of the separated flow.

Developing the structured near-wall mesh was also challenging because the required y+ condition had to be balanced against mesh skewness and orthogonality. Several bias factors were tested before achieving an acceptable first-layer height and overall mesh quality.

The analysis indicated that future accuracy could be improved by using the k–ω SST turbulence model with a finer near-wall mesh and a y+ value close to one. Rotating the aerofoil geometry instead of changing the inlet-velocity direction would also provide a more physically consistent boundary-condition arrangement for different angles of attack.
