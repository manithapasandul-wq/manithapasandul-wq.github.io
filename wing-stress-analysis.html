Structures Project

Experimental and Numerical Stress Analysis of a Rectangular Wing

Department of Mechanical Engineering · University of Moratuwa

This project investigated how the location of a concentrated landing-gear-type load affects stress distribution within a rectangular wing structure. Two loading conditions were considered:

  • 30 lb load applied at the front-spar tip
  • 30 lb load applied at the rear-spar tip

The structural response was studied using both experimental strain measurements and finite element analysis. The main objective was to compare the load transfer through the spars, ribs, and skin panels and determine the more suitable spar for landing-gear attachment.

Experimental Stress Analysis

The wing prototype was mounted as a cantilever using the TrueStructures strain measurement system. Uniaxial strain gauges were positioned on the front-spar flanges and rear-spar web, while three rectangular strain-gauge rosettes were installed on the front-spar web and upper and lower wing skins.

The unloaded and loaded voltage readings were recorded for both loading cases. The measurements were converted into strain and stress values using the gauge factor and aluminium material properties. The rosette measurements were processed using strain-transformation relationships and Mohr’s circle to determine:

  • Principal strains.
  • Principal stresses.
  • Principal directions.
  • Maximum in-plane shear stress.

For both loading cases, the highest measured stress occurred at gauge B on the lower flange of the front spar. The maximum measured stress was approximately:

  • 69.62 MPa for front-spar loading.
  • 58.16 MPa for rear-spar loading.

Moving the load to the rear spar reduced the maximum experimentally measured stress by approximately 16.5%. It also changed the internal load path, increasing the contribution of the rear-spar web while reducing stress in several front-spar and skin locations.

CAD Modelling and Finite Element Analysis

A detailed three-dimensional model of the wing structure was developed in SolidWorks. The model included:

  • Front and rear spars.
  • Upper and lower skins.
  • Internal ribs.
  • Lightening holes.
  • Spar flanges.
  • Root mounting holes.

Small details such as rivets, minor fillets, and non-structural features were removed from the numerical model to improve mesh quality and reduce computational demand. Aluminium 6061-T6 was assigned to the complete structure.

The model was imported into ANSYS Static Structural and meshed using tetrahedral elements. A mesh-independence study was completed by varying the element sizes assigned to the spars, ribs, and skin. Maximum deformation, equivalent strain, and equivalent stress were monitored during refinement. A mesh containing approximately 171,817 elements was selected for the final analysis.

The wing-root bolt holes were modelled as fixed supports to represent the experimental clamping arrangement. Separate simulations were conducted by applying the 133.45 N vertical load to the front- and rear-spar tips.

Experimental and Numerical Validation

The rear-spar loading case produced approximately 2.8% greater deformation and 3.6% greater maximum equivalent stress. However, these numerical maxima were influenced by local stress concentrations near the load application and fixed-support regions.

Validation

The maximum experimental and numerical stresses were compared to assess the validity of the finite element model.

For front-spar loading the numerical maximum stress differed from the experimental maximum by approximately 7.3%, showing good agreement for a preliminary structural validation. For rear-spar loading the difference was approximately 33.1%.

The larger deviation was attributed to differences between the two methods: strain gauges measured local normal strains only at selected locations, while ANSYS reported the maximum equivalent stress anywhere in the model. The numerical maximum may therefore have occurred near a bolt hole, edge, or concentrated load point where no strain gauge was installed.

Despite these differences, both methods provided useful and complementary information. The experiment identified the measured load distribution at selected structural locations, while FEA showed the complete deformation and stress field of the wing.

Outcome

The experimental results indicated that applying the load to the rear spar reduced the maximum measured stress and produced a more favourable stress distribution across most gauge locations. The finite element model predicted only a small increase in overall deformation and maximum equivalent stress for rear-spar loading.

Based on the combined results, the rear spar was recommended as the more suitable landing-gear mounting location for the prototype wing. A load-spreading bracket and local reinforcement should be incorporated around the attachment region to control local stress concentration.

Key Work Completed

The project involved:

  • Conducting strain-gauge testing under two spar-loading conditions.
  • Processing uniaxial and rosette measurements.
  • Calculating principal stresses using Mohr’s circle.
  • Developing the detailed wing structure in SolidWorks.
  • Creating and simplifying the ANSYS finite element model.
  • Performing a mesh-independence study.
  • Applying representative loading and support conditions.
  • Comparing experimental and numerical stress results.
  • Assessing load transfer through the spars and wing skins.
  • Recommending a suitable landing-gear mounting location.

The work strengthened practical experience in aircraft structural analysis, strain measurement, stress transformation, SolidWorks modelling, ANSYS Static Structural, mesh convergence, and experimental validation.
